Interview notes

Questions I’ve been asked while applying for data jobs, what I said at the time, and what I’d say now. Writing them down is how I’m learning from them.

business case

Declining e‑commerce sales

If a company’s in-store sales are stable but its e-commerce sales are dropping, what are the first things you would look at?

what I said

Check whether anything inside the business had changed, especially on the online platform compared with the physical stores. If nothing had, look at changes in customer behaviour or market conditions.

what I’d add now
  • First check the drop is real, not broken tracking or a reporting change
  • Split the funnel: fewer visitors, fewer people buying, or smaller baskets?
  • Then slice it by device, channel, region and product to see where it’s happening
  • Only then look outside: competitors, prices, seasonality
business case

Which customers to prioritise

A textile company sells materials like silk and wool to customers such as Zara and H&M. It wants to keep or strengthen these relationships. How would you decide which ones to prioritise?

what I said

Build a forecasting model to predict the potential revenue from each customer, and use those predictions to help prioritise.

what I’d add now
  • Revenue isn’t everything: margin, growth and the risk of losing a customer matter too
  • Start with a simple score across those before building a model
  • Ask the sales team what they already know about each customer
SQL

Customer spending in August

Write a SQL query that groups sales by customer and calculates how much each customer spent in August.

what I’d write 
SELECT customer_id,
       SUM(amount) AS total_spent
FROM sales
WHERE sale_date >= '2026-08-01'
  AND sale_date <  '2026-09-01'
GROUP BY customer_id
ORDER BY total_spent DESC;

Using a date range instead of “month = 8” means you don’t mix up Augusts from different years, and the database can use an index on the date.

metrics

Accuracy, precision and recall

What do accuracy, precision and recall mean, and when would you use each?

my notes
  • Accuracy: the share of predictions that are right. Fine when classes are balanced, misleading when they aren’t (99% accuracy is easy if 99% of cases are negative)
  • Precision: of everything you flagged as positive, how much really was. Use it when false alarms are costly, like a spam filter binning real emails
  • Recall: of all the real positives, how many you caught. Use it when missing one is costly, like screening for a disease
time series

Evaluating a forecasting model

How would you evaluate a time-series forecasting model? We talked about metrics like mean absolute error.

my notes
  • Never shuffle. Train on the past, test on the future, ideally with rolling (walk-forward) splits
  • Compare against a dumb baseline, like “same as last week”. If you can’t beat it, the model isn’t helping
  • MAE is easy to explain (average error in real units). RMSE punishes big misses more. Be careful with MAPE when values get close to zero
  • Watch for leakage: features that quietly use information from the future
modelling

Simple or complex model?

When would you use a simple model rather than a more complex one? Which hyperparameters would you tune?

my notes
  • Start simple. Go for it when data is small, features are many, or people need to understand the model. In my epigenetic clock, Elastic Net beat LightGBM and a neural network
  • Go complex when there’s lots of data, clearly non-linear patterns, and the extra accuracy is worth losing some interpretability
  • Hyperparameters: regularisation strength (and the L1/L2 mix) for linear models; tree depth, learning rate, number of trees and minimum samples per leaf for boosting
  • Tune with cross-validation, for example with Optuna, and keep an eye on overfitting
